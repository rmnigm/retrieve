Where SilverTorch can reach the recall you ask for, it beats exact LiNR at every size

Two families of filtered GPU retrieval on one A100: SilverTorch (Meta's official code, adapted by us) probes a few clusters of an int8 index; LiNR (V1 mask-then-score, V2 gather-then-score, our implementation from the paper) scans every item exactly. Every SilverTorch point here was timed in the same process as LiNR, from 0.8 M to 30 M items.

ⓘCitable, seed 0. Both measurement gates are green. Recall here means fidelity to exact search: the share of the exact filtered top-k (highest inner products over the whole catalogue, fp32, same filter) that a method returns. It is not the papers' end-task recall, but on Goodreads the two move together (Pearson 0.88–0.94). Cells unstable in every run, and LiNR on YFCC-synth at k 1000 (fp16 storage), are marked †.
1 · The tuned point per dataset size

Give SilverTorch the probes it needs, and it still wins wherever it can get there

How to read it: each row is a real filter, smallest index at the top. A dot is how many times faster SilverTorch is than the faster of LiNR V1 and V2 at the fastest setting that reaches the recall target, at one query (hollow), 16 and 64. Hatched rows never reach the target, even at a quarter of the clusters probed, so exact LiNR wins by default. That is a recall ceiling, not a speed loss.

2 · The paper's production setting

At 24 probes SilverTorch is always faster, but it returns a different answer

How to read it: the same filters at SilverTorch's default of 24 probes. Left: times faster than the best exact LiNR at one query (hollow) and 16 (filled). Right: recall@100. The speed-up grows with N and batch; the recall falls with N and with filter selectivity. A reference row, not a comparison at equal quality.

3 · The price of recall

Higher targets cost probes; broad filters at large batches are where LiNR V1 fights back

How to read it: synthetic filters with a controlled pass rate. The blue lines are SilverTorch at 24 probes (light) and at the fastest setting reaching 0.90 and 0.95 (darker); a missing point means that target was not reached. At the most selective filter it often is not; at broad filters with a batch of 64, 0.95 can cost more than LiNR V1's full scan (LAION 3 M synth: 2.7×).

4 · Every cell at matched recall

Who is faster at equal recall, filter by filter

How to read it: a number is SilverTorch's time as a fraction of the best LiNR's at the tuned point (0.30 = 3.3× faster). Hatched: SilverTorch never reaches the target, so LiNR wins by default. Solid orange: SilverTorch reaches it but LiNR is faster.

5 · Inside LiNR

LiNR's V2 beats V1 only for one query at a time

How to read it: below the line V2 (gather the passing items, then score) is faster than V1 (score everything, mask). At bs 1 V2 wins below a pass rate of about 0.8–0.97, by more as N grows. At bs 16 the crossover drops to about 0.02; at bs 64 and 256, V1 wins on every real filter, and V2 at bs 256 times out on PubMed and YFCC.

6 · Co-design

Meta's "probe, then filter" pays off only at 10–30 M items

How to read it: above 1, filtering inside the probed clusters (the paper's co-design) beats building the filter over the whole index first. With Meta's code as published, the paper's 1.79–2.15× shows up at 30 M; below 10 M the full-index path is faster. The adapted code is roughly neutral at bs 1 and gains 1.1–1.6× at bs 64 (median).
