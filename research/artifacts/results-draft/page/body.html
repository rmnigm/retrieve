SilverTorch and LiNR under filtering: experimental results

Two families of filtered GPU retrieval on one A100. SilverTorch (Meta's official code, adapted by us) probes a few clusters of an int8 index and filters them with a bloom filter, which lets some false positives through. LiNR (our implementation from the paper) filters exactly: V1 scores every item, then masks; V2 gathers the passing items, then scores them; V3 ranks everything with 1-bit codes, then rescores a candidate pool. Five datasets, from 0.8 M to 30 M items, and synthetic filters with a controlled pass rate.

Recall here measures fidelity to exact search: the share of the exact filtered top-k (the highest inner products over the whole catalogue, under the same filter) that a method returns. A bloom false positive in the top-k counts as a miss. This is not the papers' end-task recall, but . Times are medians per batch, with SilverTorch and LiNR timed in the same process.

1

Quality, speed and memory under filtering

Table 1 shows each method on one real filter per dataset, at k = 100. SilverTorch appears at its default of 24 probes and at the fastest setting that reaches recall 0.90 and 0.95. LiNR V3 appears at the smallest pool that reaches the same targets. Speed-up is against exact LiNR V1. The default view is PubMed's MeSH filter at a batch of 16.

The results show three main patterns. First, at 24 probes SilverTorch is 3–58× faster than V1 at a batch of 16, but it returns a different answer: recall@100 is 0.33–0.89. Its time barely moves with catalogue size (0.19–0.29 ms per batch from 0.8 M to 30 M items), while V1's grows with it (0.57 ms on Goodreads, 16.8 ms on LAION 30M), consistent with SilverTorch scanning only 24 of 512–4,096 clusters and V1 scoring every item. Second, held to recall 0.90, SilverTorch still wins by a wide margin wherever a modest share of clusters is enough: 15× V1 on PubMed's MeSH filter, 7.0× on LAION 30M's tag filter, 6.7× on arXiv's main category. Where it must probe half the clusters (LAION's domain filter at 1–10 M items, its tag filter at 1 M), it lands between 0.7× and 1.4× of V1's speed. Third, among LiNR's variants exact V1 is the fastest at a batch of 16 on every filter but one. The exception is PubMed's MeSH filter, where only 0.02 % of items pass and V2 halves the time, consistent with it gathering just those. V3 at a pool reaching 0.90 beats the faster exact variant only on LAION's domain filter, by 2–7 %. Memory is covered in section 6.

Speed at equal recall

Table 2 holds SilverTorch to a recall target and compares it with the fastest LiNR that reaches the same target: exact V1 or V2, or V3 at its smallest sufficient pool. Each cell shows how many times faster SilverTorch is; a value below 1 means LiNR is faster. "Share" is the fraction of clusters SilverTorch probes at that setting. The table covers the real filters at k = 100; the full grid (k = 100, 1000, 2048, plus the synthetic filters) is below it.

Three patterns stand out. First, the share of clusters SilverTorch must probe decides the outcome. Over all 276 cells, 183 reach the target within a quarter of the clusters, and there SilverTorch beats the best LiNR in 181, with a median of 0.29 of its time (about 3.4× faster). Another 51 cells reach the target only by probing half to three quarters of the clusters, and SilverTorch wins 13 of those. The other 42 do not reach it even at three quarters. Second, the hard cases are filters whose passing items are thinly spread across clusters: LAION's domain filter, and its tag filter on the small LAION subsets. This is consistent with the classic shortage of candidates: a query's nearest clusters hold few items that pass, so recall has to be bought with more probes, while an exact scan pays the same price whatever the filter. Catalogue size matters for the same reason (section 4). Third, SilverTorch's lead narrows at a batch of 64 (on LAION 30M's tag filter: 7.4× at one query, 4.4× at 64). This is consistent with V1's full scan being one matrix product shared by the whole batch, while each SilverTorch query probes its own clusters and so amortises less.

Effect of batch size on speed

Table 3 gives the time per query (time per batch ÷ batch size) at batches of 1, 16 and 64.

Batching helps the three methods very differently. LiNR V1's cost per query falls 13× on arXiv from one query to 64 (0.83 to 0.06 ms) and 34× on PubMed, consistent with it reading the whole index once per batch. V2's falls only about 2×, consistent with it gathering the passing items separately for each query. For one query, V2 is faster than V1 below a pass rate of 0.85–0.97; at a batch of 16 the crossover drops to 0.014–0.065, and at 64 V1 wins on every filter (Figure 2). For one query SilverTorch takes about 0.19 ms at most settings on every dataset, consistent with a fixed cost per call rather than the work done; batching is then nearly free until the probed work dominates: at 24 probes on arXiv its cost per query falls 34×, to 6 µs.

Scaling with catalogue size

LAION runs at 1, 3, 10 and 30 M items with the same two filters, which isolates the effect of size. Table 4 shows SilverTorch at recall 0.90 against the best LiNR, and at 24 probes against V1. Table 5 shows Meta's co-design: filtering only inside the probed clusters, against building the filter over the whole index first. It is timed on Meta's code as published, because the adapted code has no whole-index path.

The results show three patterns. First, at a fixed setting SilverTorch's lead grows in proportion to the catalogue: at 24 probes it is 3× V1 at 1 M and 55–58× at 30 M, while its own time stays near 0.2 ms. Second, at equal recall the lead also grows, from 0.4–1.0× at 1 M to 4.4–7.4× at 30 M on the tag filter. The share of clusters needed for 0.90 falls from a half to an eighth. With the cluster counts used, a cluster at 30 M holds about 1,800 items against about 120 at 1 M. Under the tag filter (0.27 % pass) that is, on average, about five passing items per probed cluster instead of 0.3, consistent with far fewer probes being needed to find 100 passing neighbours. Third, the co-design pays only at 10–30 M items: 1.6× at a batch of 16 and 3.2× at 64 at 30 M, against the paper's 1.79–2.15×, and about even or slower at 1–3 M. That is consistent with the whole-index filter being cheap when the index is small, so skipping it saves little.

Robustness to filter selectivity

The synthetic filters pass 0.1 %, 1 %, 10 % or 100 % of the items at random, so selectivity is the only thing that changes. Table 6 shows a batch of 16.

The methods fall into three groups. Exact V1 takes the same time at every pass rate (16.4 ms on LAION-synth 30M), consistent with it scoring every item and masking afterwards. Exact V2 costs grow with the pass rate, consistent with it gathering the passing items: on LAION-synth 30M it takes 14 ms at 0.1 % and 99 ms with no filter; on PubMed-synth, 5 ms and 146 ms. V2 is the right choice only for very selective filters. SilverTorch behaves as the shortage of candidates predicts. At 24 probes its recall falls from 0.77 with no filter to 0.36 at 0.1 %, consistent with fewer of the items in its nearest clusters passing. Restoring 0.90 costs more probes at the rarest filter (1.9 ms against 1.2 ms at 1–10 %), but that is still 9–14× under V1.

Index memory and build time

Table 7 gives the GPU index size per method and SilverTorch's build time, from its smallest to its largest number of clusters.

SilverTorch's index is 0.54–0.72 of LiNR's, consistent with storing each vector in int8 (one byte per dimension against fp16's two) plus a 1,024-bit (128-byte) bloom filter per item. That overhead weighs most at d = 128 (0.72 on Goodreads) and least at d = 768 (0.54 on PubMed). V3 adds its 1-bit codes to V1's index (+4–6 %). More clusters cost build time, not memory: 65,536 clusters make the index only 0.5 % larger than 4,096, but take 17× longer to build (105 s against 1,738 s at 30 M).

7

Exact or probabilistic filter

To isolate the price of SilverTorch's bloom filter, the same search ran with an exact filter fused in its place, at the same clusters and probes. Table 8 gives the bloom's time as a share of the exact filter's: below 1, the bloom is faster. The two filters come from separate runs on the same GPU, and recall is identical for both.

The picture is mixed. First, the bloom is faster where the filter is expensive to check exactly: PubMed's MeSH filter (0.71–0.87 of the exact time) and LAION's domain filter at deep probes (0.77–0.88). That is consistent with a few bitwise ANDs per item costing less than walking several exact clauses. Second, the exact filter is as fast or faster on LAION's four-tag filter when clusters are small: 1.1–1.6× with 120–370 items per cluster, about even at 450–600. With 1,800 items per cluster (LAION 30M) the bloom is faster again (0.88). That is consistent with the bloom paying a fixed cost per probed cluster, which dominates when clusters are small; the exact kernel reads only 5–24 bytes per item. At 24 probes on LAION 3M the exact filter is also about 10 % faster, consistent with there being little probed work for the bloom to save. On arXiv and Goodreads the two are within 6 %. Third, the bloom's approximation costs nothing measurable at Meta's default width: Narrower filters let more through: at 64 bits, 2.3 % of PubMed's failing items pass, and recall at a fixed setting falls from 0.69 to 0.50.

Sensitivity to parameters

Table 9 varies SilverTorch's number of clusters on LAION 30M: index size, build time, recall at an eighth and a quarter of the clusters probed, and the fastest timed setting that reaches 0.90 at a batch of 16. Table 10 varies LiNR V3's candidate pool, as a share of the items passing the filter.

For SilverTorch, recall follows the share of clusters probed, not their count. Finer clustering raises recall slightly at the same share (+0.03 at an eighth on the tag filter) but needs four times the probes for that share, consistent with each probe carrying a fixed cost. The middle setting, 16,384 clusters (about three times √N), is the fastest to reach 0.90: 2.4 ms, against 3.9 ms at 4,096 and 3.3 ms at 65,536. The best number of clusters sits near the usual √N rule of thumb. For LiNR V3, recall rises with the pool until the pool holds nearly every passing item. The pool never drops below 2,000 candidates, which is why recall is flat at small shares. On PubMed's MeSH filter (0.02 % pass), even that floor holds all passing items, so recall is 0.999 from the smallest pool. On arXiv's main category (13.6 % pass), 0.90 needs 10 % of the passing items.

Seed to seed

The timed comparison was rerun at seeds 1 and 2: a new clustering, a new synthetic draw and a new timing run. Seed 2 ran on a different machine from seeds 0–1, with the same GPU model. Table 11 gives the spread over the seeds at the same setting, with each tuned cell held at the setting picked at seed 0. Table 12 checks the machines against each other on LiNR V1, whose time does not depend on the clustering.

The comparison barely moves. At the same setting, SilverTorch's time varies by a median % per dataset, and recall by at most . Held at its seed-0 setting, SilverTorch's time over the best LiNR's spreads by a median %, and of the tuned cells change winner. LiNR V1 stays within % of its seed-0 time on every machine. SilverTorch's shortest times are another matter: the settings under 0.25 ms, close to its fixed cost per call, run 5–9 % slower at seed 2 on Goodreads, arXiv, their synthetic sets and LAION 30M, whose seed 2 ran on a different machine from seeds 0–1. The cells moved between the other two machines show no such shift. This is consistent with the machines differing in fixed cost per call, not in GPU throughput, and it is the main source of spread on those datasets; it changes no winner. The largest spreads in SilverTorch's time come from PubMed's MeSH filter at deep probes (5.9–9.1 ms at a batch of 64), consistent with a new clustering changing how many passing items the probed clusters hold. Re-picking the setting at each seed instead changes one winner (). That is a pick sitting exactly on the target, not a change in speed. Seeds 0–2 cover every real dataset and the synthetic PubMed and LAION 10M sets. Synthetic Goodreads and arXiv have seeds 0 and 2, and synthetic LAION 30M has seed 0 so far, with seed 1 still running (it gets no seed 2). LAION 1M's deep settings ran at seed 0 only, by design.

10

Conclusions

The experiments support three conclusions about filtered retrieval on a GPU.

  1. SilverTorch is the faster method wherever it reaches the required recall within about a quarter of its clusters. It is several times faster than the best LiNR at recall 0.90, and its lead grows with catalogue size, up to 7× at 30 M items. Its index is also 28–46 % smaller. Its weakness is a shortage of candidates: filters whose passing items are thinly spread across clusters force it to probe half the index or more, and there exact LiNR is as fast or faster.
  2. LiNR V1 is the right exact method for batches: its time per query falls fastest with batch size, and from a batch of 16 it beats V2 on all but the rarest filters. LiNR V2 wins for one query at a time and for very selective filters, consistent with gathering a few passing items costing less than scoring all of them. LiNR V3 rarely beats the faster exact variant at equal recall.
  3. The bloom filter is a sound engineering choice at Meta's default width. It is faster than a well-built exact filter where the filter is expensive to check, at no measurable cost in recall. It is not faster everywhere, and the co-design around it pays only at 10 M items and above.
