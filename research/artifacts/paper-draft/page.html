Reproducibility study · model-based retrieval · LiNR (CIKM '24) and SilverTorch (SIGIR '26)

Industrial retrieval claims are regime claims

Both papers do model-based retrieval: filtered retrieval as operations inside the model graph on the GPU, not a separate nearest-neighbour service. We reimplemented LinkedIn's LiNR and Meta's SilverTorch from their papers, ran Meta's released kernels beside ours, and tested every claim we could on five public datasets from 0.8 M to 30 M items. Most claims hold where the papers measured them: tens of millions of items, batched queries, multi-term filters. Several flip at the sizes public benchmarks use. Three knobs decide which: how many items, how many queries per batch, and how selective the filter is for each query.

Exploration numbers: a final pass at one frozen code version reruns every cell before anything is cited. Timings compare only within one machine, ratios inside one interleaved run; recall is top-100 against an exact filtered oracle. Meta's numbers use its default fp16 score path unless marked int32. Most of our IVF timings come from a kernel version two bit-exact updates old; those updates only make our batched timings faster.

Knob 1

Scale: the papers' wins appear at tens of millions of items

Both papers measured on 15–80 M item production indexes. Below a few million, exact search is cheap, fixed overheads dominate, and several of their trade-offs reverse.

IVF's edge over exact search shrinks as the index grows

IVF recall at the paper's 24 probes
Lines: uniform synthetic filters. Diamonds: each dataset's broadest real filter. Index sizes are tuned per dataset (1,024–16,384 lists).
IVF speed-up over the cheaper exact scan at recall 0.95
Every real filter, IVF at its dataset's tuned probe count, same machine and code. Grey: IVF stays below 0.95 (best recall in the tooltip).

At a fixed 24 probes IVF scans ever less of a growing index, and its recall falls. Matching 0.95 recall eats its speed: on 0.8–3 M items IVF is 3.8–4.8× faster than exact search at batch 16, at 30 M only 1.3×. With a single query on 0.8 M items exact search costs a quarter of a millisecond and IVF saves almost nothing.

Most real filters never reach 0.95 at the tuned probe count: 4 of 12 do. At 10 M items none does, on YFCC or PubMed.

Co-design pays off only at 30 M with multi-term filters

Full-mask time ÷ co-design time (above 1 = co-design faster)
One dot per interleaved pair (filter × probes × batch). Meta eager, ours CUDA graph. Shaded: the paper's 1.79–2.15×. Recall is identical on every pair.

SilverTorch's co-design filters only the probed clusters instead of the whole index. That saves work in proportion to the index, the batch and the number of filter terms, against a fixed per-call cost. At 30 M with a four-term filter both implementations reach the paper's ~2× (Meta 2.1×, ours 2.4× at batch 16) and more at batch 64. Below 3 M Meta's version is 14–21 % slower with co-design, and ours gains nothing.

LiNR's 1-bit V3 pays off at 10 M items and moderate pass rates

At 10 M items with 1.8 % passing, V3 is 21 % faster than exact V2 at batch 16 with recall 0.990, the gain LiNR claims. Where nearly everything passes, the full scan V1 is the one to beat: V3 wins 3.9× unbatched and loses batched. At 0.8 M and at very low pass rates there is no gain, and fewer bits than dimensions cost recall fast.

Knob 2

Batch size: single-query and batched serving rank the methods differently

A single query is bound by launches and fixed costs; a batch is bound by the work per item. The comparisons below come out differently for the two, and some reverse.

LiNR V1 vs V2: the crossover exists only for batches, and moves with scale

V2 time ÷ V1 time, batch 16 (above 1 = full scan V1 faster)
Lines: uniform synthetic filters at nine pass rates, one interleaved run per size. Diamonds: real filters at their overall pass rate.
V2 time ÷ V1 time, one query

With fused kernels the gather-first V2 wins at low pass rates everywhere. In batches the full scan V1 takes over above p ≈ 0.27 on 0.8–3 M items and above 0.12–0.14 on 10–30 M; for single queries V2 wins at every pass rate up to 3 M and loses only near the unfiltered end from 10 M. LiNR's 15.5 M setting sits at our 10–30 M crossover.

0.8 and 30 M ran on one machine, 3 and 10 M on another; 3 M agrees within 0.01 across both. At 10 M three seeds put the batch-16 crossover at p 0.117 (95 % CI 0.114–0.119). Item count and dimension grow together (d128 → d256).

A custom filter kernel beats compiled PyTorch in batches, not on single queries

torch.compile time ÷ our Triton time, LiNR V1 on real filters (above 1 = ours faster)
max-autotune compile of the same V1 ops, eager over eager (the compiled arm replays CUDA graphs itself), top-100; recall equal in every cell.

LiNR attributes a ~100× speed-up to its custom filter kernel over native masking. Against plain PyTorch we see 1.4–12×. Against torch.compile, which fuses the mask into one masked matrix-vector product, our kernel wins 1.6–1.8× in batches on most filters, loses for single queries below 10 M items (compiled takes 0.54–0.64 of our time) and on selective multi-term filters in batches, and ties at 10 M. A single-query kernel now in its gate runs in 0.57–0.77 of our current time.

Meta's time ÷ ours, so above 1 means ours is faster. Meta runs its probe list, bloom search and scoring as separate kernels with host work between them; ours fuses them. Ours is 1.4–2.1× faster at d128, end to end and on device time alone, 2.0–2.2× at d768 with the paper's 24 probes and 1.1–1.7× with 1,024, and 1.5–2.2× across the real-filter sweeps. The exception is 30 M with 128 probes at batch 64: Meta's fp16 path is 6–26 % faster there, and against its int32 path, which scores exactly as ours does, the two are even. Meta's path cannot be captured in a CUDA graph; ours in graph mode is another 4–7× faster at d128.

Knob 3

The filter: what matters is each query's own pass rate

Both papers describe filters by their overall pass rate. Per query, real filters spread over orders of magnitude, and IVF and V2 both respond to the per-query value.

IVF recall follows the local pass rate, not the global one

Recall vs pass rate, arXiv 3 M
Uniform: items pass at random. Correlated: the filter follows the embedding's clusters. Same index (2,048 lists).
What predicts per-query recall (Spearman ρ)
Local pass rate: share of a query's unfiltered top-100 that pass. 24 probes, real filters.

At the same overall pass rate, a uniform filter collapses IVF recall while a cluster-aligned one leaves it flat. The share of a query's own neighbours that pass predicts its recall far better on both real datasets. Selective filters need more probes only when they cut across the clusters: a hundred times lower pass rate needs about 3.3× the probes, far less than 1/p.

The same spread moves LiNR's crossover on real filters (diamonds in the V1/V2 chart): in a batch, V2 costs as much as its heaviest query, so a spread-out filter crosses at a lower overall pass rate.

What the papers do not say

Mechanisms behind the numbers

The recall ceiling is SilverTorch's single global int8 scale

Recall@100 by item-score precision on the same probed lists
Each bench at its best tuned point (up to a quarter of the index); YFCC at 256 probes. Only the item score precision changes.

SilverTorch reports that int8 IVF cannot reach 0.95 recall but not why. Holding the probed lists fixed and switching item scores to fp16 lifts recall to 0.97–1.00 on every bench where the filter is broad; per-row int8 scales recover a third to half of the loss. The loss grows with the embedding's score resolution: a few points at d128, nearly half the recall on YFCC's CLIP vectors. Meta's released kernel already carries an unused per-embedding scale.

Only very selective filters keep a residual from the scan limit: PubMed's 0.02 % filter reaches 0.976 with fp16 and a quarter of the index probed.

Bloom filters: far fewer false positives than reported, and Meta's differ from ours

Bloom false-positive rate vs bits per item (5 hashes)
Our transposed bloom on real attributes; a missing point means no false positive observed. Grey: the paper's two figures at 1,024 bits, read as percent.

On arXiv and goodreads no false positive survives from 128 bits; on PubMed's rich MeSH attributes the rate falls more than tenfold per doubling, to 6×10⁻⁸ at 1,024 bits, with latency flat in width. SilverTorch's two figures at 1,024 bits disagree with each other. Meta's released bloom is built per 2,048-document bundle and admits false positives ours rejects: at 30 M it displaced 20 true top-100 items over 283 of 9,722 queries, so end-to-end ids with bloom filters differ by design while the scorer itself matches bit for bit.

Open points

Where Meta's code and the papers' text stand

Scope. We compare what can run inside a model graph: plain and compiled PyTorch, both papers' methods, and Meta's kernels. Standalone nearest-neighbour libraries such as Faiss, cuVS or HNSW are services outside the graph, so they are not baselines here, although SilverTorch compares against them.

Meta's released code. It cannot run in a CUDA graph (its ops synchronise with the host inside), ignores the bloom width setting, builds its bloom per document bundle, and compiles its host code unoptimised; an optimised build of the same kernels gives identical outputs in 0.77–0.98 of the time.

The papers' text. SilverTorch's two bloom false-positive figures at 1,024 bits cannot both hold. LiNR's V3 bit budget reads as one bit per dimension in one place and 512 bits on d128 in another; we run one bit per dimension.

How we time. Kernels only inside the timed call, preparation measured apart; ratios from interleaved runs in one process; comparisons within one machine; clock speeds, which cannot be locked in the container, sampled and flagged.

Routing by local pass rate. Sending low-local-pass-rate queries to exact search picks exactly the queries IVF fails, but the router's own cost kept it off the speed–recall front everywhere we measured; it is not in the library.
