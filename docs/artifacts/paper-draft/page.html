Reproducibility study · LiNR (CIKM '24) and SilverTorch (SIGIR '26)

Filtered GPU retrieval, reproduced: what holds, what breaks, what is new

We reimplemented LinkedIn's LiNR and Meta's SilverTorch in Triton from the papers' text, ran Meta's released kernels next to ours, and tested every claim we could on five public datasets from 0.8 M to 30 M items on one A100.

These are exploration numbers. A final pass at one frozen code version reruns every cell before anything is cited. Every timing comparison here is read inside one machine and, where it is a ratio, inside one interleaved run. Recall is at top-100 against an exact filtered fp32 oracle.

Reproduces

  • LiNR's V1/V2 trade-off: the full scan wins at high pass rates, the gather-first scan at low ones.
  • SilverTorch's int8 IVF tops out below 0.95 recall, and unaligned selective filters need more probes.
  • Co-design's ~1.8× speed-up, at the paper's own scale and filter complexity, in Meta's code and in ours.
  • Our Triton kernels match Meta's int32 path bit for bit.

Does not reproduce

  • The ~100× gap between a custom filter kernel and native PyTorch masking. We see 1.4–12×.
  • V3's "keep 1 %, gain ~10 %" at low pass rates. It pays only when most items pass.
  • Co-design below a few million items: Meta's code is 14–21 % slower with it.
  • The bloom false-positive figures: the paper's two numbers disagree, and ours are far lower.

New

  • IVF recall follows each query's local pass rate, not the global one.
  • SilverTorch's recall ceiling comes from its single global int8 scale. Per-row scales recover half the loss, fp16 nearly all.
  • At 30 M with selective real filters, IVF needs a quarter of the index to reach 0.95 recall and is then only 1.3× faster than exact filtered search, against 3–11× at 0.8–3 M.
  • Meta's released path is host-bound. An independent 600-line Triton version is faster end to end in every cell we measure.

New take · C6

IVF recall follows the local pass rate, not the global one

Recall vs pass rate, arXiv 3 M
SilverTorch (our Triton), 2048 lists. Uniform = each item passes at random; correlated = the filter follows the embedding's clusters.
What predicts per-query recall (Spearman ρ)
Local pass rate = share of a query's unfiltered top-100 that pass. 24 probes, real filters.

With the same pass rate, the same index and the same number of probes, a uniform filter collapses IVF recall at low pass rates while a cluster-aligned one leaves it flat. The global pass rate cannot tell the two apart; the share of a query's own neighbours that pass can, and it predicts per-query recall far better on both real datasets.

The probes needed for 0.95 grow far slower than 1/p: a hundred times lower pass rate needs about 3.3× the probes. Real filters fall between the two synthetic curves, placed by how aligned they are with the embedding.

Scale · C6

As N grows, IVF gets faster than exact search but loses recall; at matched recall its edge shrinks to 1.3× at 30 M

IVF recall at the paper's 24 probes
Each point is its own dataset and index size (0.8 M d128 → 30 M d256). Real filters: broadest kept sweep per dataset.
IVF speed-up over exact search at recall 0.95
Batch 16, CUDA graph. Below 1 = exact is faster. Hollow bars: IVF never reaches 0.95 within a quarter of the index; the bar shows the best recall instead (right axis label).

At a fixed 24 probes IVF scans ever less of a growing index, so its raw speed edge over a full scan widens from about 5× at 0.8 M to about 80× at 30 M. Its recall falls in step, and matching 0.95 recall eats the edge.

At 0.8–3 M with broad filters, IVF at 0.95 is 3–11× faster than exact search; at 3 M with a 0.1 % uniform filter the two are already even. At 10 M on YFCC it never gets there. At 30 M with selective real filters it needs a quarter of the index to reach 0.95, and is then only 1.3× faster than exact V2 at batch 16 (2.8× at batch 1); where the filter is a single term it never reaches 0.95.

Each bar compares IVF and exact search timed on the same machine. The 3 M uniform-filter bars at p 0.01 and 0.001 interpolate the probe count; those bars and the 0.8 M exact time come from older kernel versions and will be re-timed on the current ones. The 30 M bar's two arms ran in the same run on current code, one after the other rather than interleaved.

Mechanism · C6

The YFCC ceiling is SilverTorch's single global int8 scale

Recall@100 on YFCC 10 M at the same 256 probes, by item precision
Same index, same probed lists; only the item score precision changes. Uniform synthetic filters.

On YFCC's CLIP embeddings, more probes stop helping early: recall sits near 0.55–0.76 whatever the probe count. Holding the probes fixed and changing only item precision moves it: per-row int8 scales recover about half the gap and fp16 nearly all of it.

SilverTorch states the ceiling but not its cause. Its released kernel already carries an unused per-embedding scale, which is the fix.

C5

Co-design pays off only at the paper's scale and filter complexity, in both backends

Full mask time ÷ co-design time (above 1 = co-design faster)
Each dot is one interleaved pair (sweep × probes × batch). Meta's code runs eager only; ours in CUDA graph. Shaded band: the paper's 1.79–2.15×. Recall is identical on every pair.

Meta's co-design path does extra host work per call, synchronising inside its ops. Below 3 M that fixed cost outweighs the masking it saves, and co-design is 14–21 % slower. At 30 M with a four-term filter the saved work dominates: 2.1× at batch 16, the top of the paper's 1.79–2.15×, and 4.2–5.3× at batch 64. With a single filter term the gain at batch 16 is under 1.1×.

Our own backend shows the same scale dependence. Below 10 M our kernels fold the filter test into the scorer, so there is little separate mask work to save: 0.98–1.05× at 0.8–3 M, slightly slower (0.84–0.95×) at 128 probes and batch 16, and 1.01–1.11× at 10 M. At 30 M the full mask over every item becomes the cost, and co-design gives 1.45–1.70× with one filter term and 2.4–3.4× with four at 32 probes. With 128 probes, more of the index is scanned anyway and the gain shrinks to 1.1–1.6×.

Every Meta point runs on the final adapter and Meta's optimised build. Meta's 0.8–3 M points cover one sweep per dataset (arXiv main category, goodreads genre).

C1

V1 vs V2: the crossover moves to lower pass rates as N grows

V2 time ÷ V1 time, batch 16
Above 1 = the full scan (V1) is faster. Each line is one interleaved run on one machine.
V2 time ÷ V1 time, batch 1
LiNR's own point: 15.5 M items, ~11 % passing, unfused ops, V2 3× slower than V1.

With fused kernels V2 wins at low pass rates everywhere, and V1 wins at high ones at batch 16: near p 0.27 at 3 M and near p 0.12–0.14 at 10–30 M. At batch 1 a crossover appears only from 10 M, close to the unfiltered end (p ≈ 0.87–0.96).

LiNR's 15.5 M high-pass set (11 % passing, V1 faster) sits right at our 10–30 M batch-16 crossover. Its batch-1 gap (V2 3× slower) is larger than anything we measure; the paper notes its numbers use unfused ops, and fusing narrows V2's penalty. Item count and dimension grow together in our series (d128, d192, d256).

All three synthetic lines ran on one machine with the same code; each ratio is read inside one interleaved run.

C7 · official vs ours

Meta's kernels against our Triton: ours is faster in every cell measured

Meta's time ÷ our time, d128 (above 1 = ours faster)
arXiv 3 M and goodreads 0.8 M, eager, top-100, interleaved. End to end = wall time per call; device = sum of every GPU kernel. Final adapter; Meta's extension built with optimisation.
Meta's time ÷ our time, PubMed 10 M d768
Bloom filter (0.02 % pass), 4096 lists, eager end to end, top-100, interleaved. Final adapter; Meta's extension built with optimisation (-O3, build hash recorded in every record); our kernels at v2.9.

At d128 ours is 1.4–2.1× faster end to end and 1.4–1.8× on device time alone; the gap is widest with the bloom filter, where Meta runs its probe list, bloom search and scoring as separate kernels with host work between them, and ours fuses them. Meta's path cannot be captured in a CUDA graph; ours in graph mode is a further 3.9–6.9× faster than Meta's eager.

At d768 ours is 2.0–2.2× faster at the paper's 24 probes and 1.7× at 1024 probes with batch 1. At 1024 probes with batch 16 the margin is 7 % (2.44 vs 2.61 ms, one seed): there the top-k over all ten million scores, which both sides run, is now our largest cost.

Both panels: final adapter, Meta's optimised build, one seed per cell, timed interleaved in one process. Bit-exactness against Meta's int32 path is checked separately and holds.

C2

V3's bit budget: one bit per dimension, and gains only where most items pass

V3 recall@100, goodreads d128, 1 % kept
64 bits (half the dimension) vs 128 (one per dimension).
V3 speed-up over exact V2, PubMed 10 M d768
768 bits, 1 % kept, batch 16, CUDA graph. Recall 0.990–0.998 for V3.

Our 1-bit codes give at most one bit per dimension; LiNR used 512 bits on d128. Halving the bits saves 1–3 % of the time and costs up to 26 recall points, and at d768 a third of the bits buys no speed at low pass rates.

V3 only pays where the filter keeps most of the corpus: 5.9× on the 99.9 %-pass PubMed sweep, 1.2× at 1.8 %, nothing at 0.02 %.

C4

Bloom false positives vanish far below the paper's width

Bloom false-positive rate vs bits per item (5 hashes)
Our transposed bloom index on real attributes. A missing point means no false positive among all candidates checked. Grey marks: the paper's two figures at 1024 bits, read as percent.

On arXiv and goodreads no false positive survives from 128 bits with 5 hashes; on PubMed, whose MeSH attributes are far richer, the rate drops by more than a decade per doubling and reaches 6×10⁻⁸ at 1024 bits. Latency is flat in width, as the paper says.

SilverTorch reports 0.067 at 1024 bits in one section and 0.00173 % in another; neither can be pinned without the authors.

Open points

Where Meta's released code and the papers' text stand today

Meta's code against Meta's paper. The official path cannot run in a CUDA graph (its ops sync with the host inside), ignores the bloom width setting, uses a different bloom hash from the paper's description, and adds host syncs on its co-design path. Its setup compiles the host code without optimisation; an optimised build of the same kernels gives identical outputs in 0.77–0.98 of the time.

The papers' text. SilverTorch gives two bloom false-positive figures at 1024 bits that cannot both hold. LiNR's V3 bit budget reads as one bit per dimension in one section and 512 bits on d128 in another; we run one bit per dimension and say so.

How we time. The timed call holds kernels only; query preparation is measured and reported apart. Ratios come from interleaved runs in one process, comparisons stay within one machine, and clock speeds, which cannot be locked in the container, are sampled and flagged.

Local-pass-rate routing. Sending low-local-pass-rate queries to exact search picks exactly the queries IVF fails, but the router's own overhead keeps it off the speed–recall front on every dataset we measured. It is not part of the library.
